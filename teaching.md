# Teaching and Mentoring

My teaching goal is to help students move confidently between biological intuition, environmental data, and quantitative reasoning. Ocean and environmental sciences increasingly require students to understand organisms and ecosystems while also working with code, statistics, sensors, and large datasets. I design teaching around that bridge.

## Teaching Philosophy

I teach from concrete systems toward general principles. In ecology and biological oceanography, students often encounter abstract concepts such as niches, succession, functional traits, stratification, and biogeochemical coupling. These ideas become more durable when students first see a real system: a plankton bloom, a river plume, a cross-shelf salinity intrusion, a microscopy image, or a time series from an autonomous float.

I also make quantitative work approachable by treating analysis as a way of asking better biological questions. In statistics and data-science modules, I emphasize interpretation, assumptions, uncertainty, and communication so students can connect model output back to organisms, ecosystems, and sampling design.

## Areas I Can Teach

- Introductory biology, organismal biology, ecology, marine biology, and environmental science.
- Biological oceanography, plankton ecology, marine biogeochemistry, and coastal ecosystem dynamics.
- Biostatistics, experimental design, ecological data analysis, and environmental data science.
- Computational methods for ocean and environmental sciences, including machine learning for ecological observations.
- Special topics in autonomous ocean observing, plankton imaging, harmful algal blooms, and AI-enabled ecosystem monitoring.

## Mentoring

My mentoring philosophy is structured independence. Students need room to develop ownership, and they also need clear expectations, regular feedback, and an environment where questions are welcome. I use written mentoring plans, regular one-on-one meetings, group meetings, code and data reviews, and writing checkpoints to make progress visible.

Because my research sits at the intersection of ecology, oceanography, and computation, I train students to collaborate across expertise. A student working on plankton imagery should understand enough taxonomy to evaluate labels, enough oceanography to interpret environmental context, and enough computation to know what a model can and cannot claim.

## Inclusion and Broader Impacts

My path through Vietnam, Georgia Tech, and Woods Hole shapes how I teach and mentor. Students arrive with different educational backgrounds, languages, levels of confidence, and access to informal academic knowledge. A good mentor makes expectations explicit and helps students see themselves as legitimate contributors to science.

I am especially interested in accessible computational ocean science: documented code, reusable workflows, open teaching materials, and projects that let students enter through field sampling, microscopy, coding, visualization, or literature synthesis while building broader scientific independence.
